## Lesson 1.1 — The esthetician's role and professional boundaries

### What you will learn

Explain the difference between cosmetic observation and diagnosis; describe when to pause a service; distinguish RTI, practical competency and state licensing evidence.

### Teaching text

Esthetics focuses on cosmetic skin care and client appearance. A professional first asks whether a service is appropriate, rather than deciding which product to sell. The client's request is information, not permission to ignore training limits or warning signs.

You may observe that a client reports discomfort, that an area looks irritated, or that a product previously caused a reaction. Record those observations in plain language. Do not turn them into a medical diagnosis. “Client reports stinging after a home product” is an observation. “Client has a specific disease” is a diagnostic claim that an apprentice should not make.

Three boundaries operate together. Your service must be within the applicable Indiana scope, within the supervisor's authority, and within your demonstrated training. A procedure listed in an apprenticeship work-process schedule does not remove any of those boundaries. Product directions and equipment instructions are additional requirements. If any requirement is unresolved, pause the procedure and ask the qualified supervisor.

Learning a subject, performing a service and becoming licensed are separate events. RTI documents instruction. OJL documents supervised work. A competency signoff documents observed performance against its criteria. A state license is issued through the licensing authority's process. One video, quiz or attendance entry does not replace the other records.

### Worked example

A client wants an aggressive treatment because a friend had good results. The apprentice has not been trained on that product. The correct response is: “I need my supervisor to assess whether this treatment is suitable and within today's service plan.” The apprentice does not make an exception because the client signs a waiver.

### Workbook activity

Write three sentences: one factual observation, one question for the supervisor, and one respectful explanation to the client. Use a fictional client with unexpected irritation. Identify where RTI, OJL and practical verification would each be documented.

### Recap

Observe accurately. Stay within verified authority and training. Pause when suitability is uncertain. Keep instruction, supervised work, mastery and licensing evidence separate.

## Lesson 1.2 — Consultation, consent and referral

### What you will learn

Conduct a structured consultation; explain consent in plain language; document a pause and referral without diagnosing.

### Teaching text

A useful consultation connects the client's goal with the information needed for a safe decision. Begin with an open question: “What would you like to improve about your skin-care routine?” Then ask relevant follow-up questions using the approved intake form. Explain why you are asking, protect privacy, and avoid collecting unrelated personal information.

Discuss prior reactions, current concerns, recent services and relevant product use. Follow the supervisor-approved screening process. Do not tell a client to stop prescribed treatment or change medication. When medication or a medical condition could affect the proposed service, refer the decision to the qualified supervisor and, when appropriate, the client's treating professional.

Consent is a conversation. Describe the planned service, expected cosmetic result, foreseeable discomforts, alternatives and aftercare. Avoid promises such as “This will cure your acne.” Invite questions. A client may decline or ask to stop; a signed form does not cancel that choice.

If you encounter broken skin, unexpected symptoms or another unresolved concern, stop the affected service and inform the supervisor. Do not conceal a warning sign with makeup or proceed to meet a sales target. Referral means explaining that the concern needs assessment by an appropriate licensed health professional, not telling the client what diagnosis or treatment they have.

Document the date, the client's own report, your observations, the service decision, supervisor involvement and advice actually given. Use the approved access-controlled record system. Do not place client images on a personal phone or social account.

### Worked example

During consultation, a client reports a strong reaction after a recent service elsewhere. A good note reads: “Client reports burning after a recent treatment. Planned service paused. Supervisor consulted. Client advised to seek appropriate medical assessment.” It does not name an unconfirmed condition.

### Workbook activity

Create a six-question consultation script. Underline questions that affect the decision to proceed. Write a consent explanation of no more than 100 words. Complete a fictional service-paused record without a diagnosis or identifiable personal information.

### Recap

Ask relevant questions. Explain the plan. Respect the client's choice. Escalate unresolved concerns. Record what happened, not assumptions.

## Lesson 1.3 — Ethical recommendations and continuing learning

### What you will learn

Give a relevant home-care recommendation; respond to a product question you cannot answer; use reliable professional sources.

### Teaching text

A recommendation should fit the client's stated goal, the service performed and the product's directions. It should also fit the client's ability to use the product. Selling several unfamiliar products at once can make instructions confusing. Explain what each recommended product is for, how the manufacturer directs its use and what the client should do if a concern develops.

Use cosmetic language. You may discuss cleansing or appearance without promising to treat a medical disease. Do not claim that a product is safe for every person or that a higher price guarantees a better outcome. If you do not know whether a product is suitable, say so and check with your supervisor.

Reliable learning starts with current regulator information, manufacturer instructions, safety data and evidence-based professional education. A social-media demonstration can suggest a question, but it cannot establish the legality or safety of a new procedure.

Keep a professional learning log: source, date, topic, what you learned, what remains uncertain and supervisor feedback. Do not add a technique to your service menu solely because you watched it online. New practical skills require appropriate instruction and observed verification.

### Workbook activity

Compare two fictional product recommendations: one promises a cure and uses pressure selling; the other explains a limited cosmetic purpose and checks client understanding. Rewrite the first recommendation. Add one source-check entry to your learning log.

### Recap

Recommend with a clear purpose. Avoid medical promises and sales pressure. Check uncertain claims. Document continuing education and apply it under supervision.

## Unit knowledge check

Institutional instructional gate: 80%, or four of five correct. This is not a state exam passing-score claim. Review explanations and use a reassessment after remediation.

1. A client asks for a procedure the apprentice has never been trained to perform. What is the best next step?  
A. Proceed after a waiver. B. Pause and consult the qualified supervisor. C. Copy a social video. D. Increase the price.

2. Which note is most appropriate?  
A. “Client has a diagnosed infection,” without medical evidence. B. “Client is difficult.” C. “Client reports stinging; planned service paused and supervisor consulted.” D. “Everything is fine,” without screening.

3. What does a passed knowledge quiz establish?  
A. A state license. B. All OJL hours. C. Knowledge performance on that assessment. D. Automatic completion of all 20 competencies.

4. Which recommendation is ethical?  
A. “This cures every skin condition.” B. “Buy all products or you cannot return.” C. “This guarantees permanent results.” D. “Here is the cosmetic purpose and labeled use; let us check suitability with the supervisor.”

5. A client asks whether to discontinue prescribed medication before a service. What should the apprentice do?  
A. Tell the client to stop it. B. Refer medication decisions to the treating professional and pause pending appropriate guidance. C. Ignore the question. D. Recommend half the dose.

### Instructor answer key

1 B — Consent does not replace training or authority.  
2 C — It documents the report, action and escalation without diagnosis.  
3 C — Knowledge assessment is only one part of completion evidence.  
4 D — Recommendations need truthful purpose, directions and suitability.  
5 B — Medication changes require the appropriate health professional.

## Supervised practical assignment and rubric

Role-play a consultation using a fictional client. No treatment or product application is required in this opening unit.

Score each criterion 0 (not demonstrated), 1 (needs coaching), or 2 (independent within the assigned task): obtains relevant information; explains scope; explains consent; identifies a reason to pause; gives a factual referral explanation; records an objective note.

Mastery for this classroom task requires 2 on each criterion. A missed safety decision requires remediation before reassessment. This rubric does not grant final occupational competency completion.

Record learner identity, date, task, assessor identity, observed evidence, feedback and reassessment result in the authorized system. Do not prefill signatures or learner credit.
